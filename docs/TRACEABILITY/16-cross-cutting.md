## 16.2 Warranty & QR verification (Spec §7/§8)

**Built this slice: the invoice verification link and the page behind it (§16-19, §16-20).** Then **the public document link and its secure download (§16-21, §16-22)**, which reuse that shape deliberately so the application tells one story about handing a file to somebody without a login: the address is derived from `document:{company}|{document}|{rotation}` and only its digest is stored, so a screen can show the address again while a read-only leak of the `documents` table publishes nothing; rotation is a version bump, withdrawal keeps its date **and takes the file back to `visibility = private`** — the document foundation already had its own rule (`allowsPublicAccessByToken`, which asks for a live token *and* public visibility), and the share flow now satisfies it rather than quietly contradicting it. A **permanent link is one with no expiry**; an expiry is explicit and bounded to a year, and the moment it passes the address is a 404 like any other. The download streams the stored bytes from the private disk with the file's own MIME type and a filename that cannot climb out of a directory, and views and downloads are recorded as different facts (`documents.public_link_viewed` versus `documents.public_file_downloaded`) with the token's hash in the access log. While wiring it, two real defects on the documents desk were found and fixed: the library page eager-loaded and printed a relation called `uploadedBy` that does not exist on the model (the page returned 500 for every user), and `Document` was missing the `branch()` relation its own `branch_id` column and branch scope imply. Two decisions carry it, and both are about not lying with a URL. **The token is derived and only its hash is stored.** A printed invoice carries a QR symbol and a reprint has to produce the same address, so the capability cannot be a value that lives only in the response of the request that created it: it is `HMAC-SHA256(APP_KEY, "verification:{company}|{invoice}|{rotation}")` base64url-encoded (43 characters, 32 bytes of entropy, no invoice number in sight), and what the row holds is `hash('sha256', $token)`. A read-only leak of the invoices table therefore publishes nothing, and a lookup compares against a digest rather than a capability. Rotation is a version bump — the old address 404s the instant the version moves and a reprint carries the new one — and withdrawal keeps the date it happened, so “this invoice was verifiable until 4 March” stays a fact. **The page publishes the document, not the books.** It names the company and branch that issued it, the number, the date, what was bought, what is paid and due and who raised it; the internal notes, the journal entry, the audit chain, costs and every other customer are never read rather than filtered out. Customer contact details are masked on the page, because a link can be forwarded or photographed off a counter. Warranty dates were deliberately absent while cover was not registered anywhere: `warranty_flag` said terms were promised on the line, and the page said exactly that rather than showing a date nobody had recorded. §16-16 has since landed, so the page now reads the register: where the goods carry a cover it names the date the cover runs to (or the date it ended), and where a line was flagged warranty with no cover row against it, it still says that instead of inventing a period. Every visit writes one `public_access_logs` row — with the token's **hash** in the `access_token` column, so the log can say which link was used without publishing a capability to whoever can read a log table — and one audit event (`sales.invoice_verified`); publishing, rotating and withdrawing each audit their own action behind the key that already owns the document's life cycle (`sales.invoices.issue`). Drafts and pending invoices are refused by the service rather than only hidden in the view: a customer verifying a draft would be verifying something the company has not asked them to pay, and a cancelled invoice verifies **as** cancelled, because the point of a verification page is that it can say what the paper cannot.

Finally **the reusable renderer, the title rule and the print history (§16-23, §16-24, §16-25)** — the slice that turns "every document type" from twelve templates into one table. Twelve document types now answer five questions in one place (`DocumentSourceRegistry`): which `document_types` row gives the paper its title, which permission a person needs to produce it, how to load the entity, what the paper says, and which papers it prints on. One shell renders them, in A4 and on 80 mm thermal, with the logo, the seal and the signature inlined so a saved copy stands alone, a watermark that the history records, label sets in English and Bengali, and a repeated table head so a long ledger paginates without losing its columns. The title is a **claim**, so the rule lives in `DocumentTitleService` and not in a template: a normal sale says INVOICE, a statutory form keeps its own title, and a non-statutory type may not borrow a tax title at all — a rule that now raises instead of printing. The same spirit governs the tax block: a zero VAT column is not printed (D10), because an empty tax column reads like a charge that was considered and waived. Every production files a copy whose checksum is the checksum of the stored bytes, bumps that document's version, and writes a history row carrying the title, the paper, the copy locale, the watermark, the user, the address and the file — so the sheet in somebody's hand can be matched against the record later. Two of the types the catalogue knows are listed as **not printable and not hidden**: a payroll sheet and a payslip need a payroll run, and §10 has employees, attendance and leave; the desk names that reason and the door answers 422 rather than printing a blank sheet. While writing the first tests that ever exercised these doors, one real defect fell out: the printing desk's own catalogue read the title service's array as an object, so the desk returned 500 for everybody — fixed here.
**And the trail itself became checkable (§16-33, §16-34, §16-35).** The audit log has been written since the first slice, and until now nothing could *check* it: the recorder built a per-company hash chain and no test had ever walked one. Two real defects came out of writing the first test that does. **The chain never verified — not once, on any row.** The recorder hashed the timestamp it was about to store (`2027-03-10 09:00:00`, the company's wall clock) while the verifier hashed what the model casts the same column back to (an ISO-8601 instant, in UTC); the two differ by six hours, so every chain failed its own verification and “tamper-evident” was a claim nothing could confirm. Both sides now pass through one canonicaliser (`AuditRecorder::stamp()` and `plain()`), which is the only way a hash written by one half of a system and read by the other is ever actually equal. **The viewer's branch limit did not hold either.** The predicate `whereIn('branch_id', [...branches, 0, null])` is not “my branches plus company-wide facts”: Laravel turns that `null` into `or branch_id is null`, which admitted every company-wide row of every actor in the company — on the one page whose whole purpose is to be trustworthy. Worse, `$attrs['branch_id'] ?? $context->branchId()` meant an *explicit* `null` — a company-wide fact — could never be recorded as one, because `??` replaced it with the acting branch. Both are fixed and both are pinned by tests, and the scope is now one grouped `where` behind the company predicate. On top of that: `AuditVocabulary` turns 286 recorded actions into 28 modules — derived from the actions themselves and checked against the source tree, rather than a hand-kept list that drifts — and translates the five names recorded before the vocabulary existed (`reconcile`, `pay`, `escalate`, `print_*`, `notify_*`) **on read only**, because those rows are hash-chained history and rewriting one would break every hash after it; a filter for `sales.cod_reconciled` finds the rows recorded as `reconcile`, and the printable report carries the reason, because a refusal without its why is not evidence of anything. `erp:audit:seal` writes down what a closed month looked like — how many events, where it started and ended, a checksum over those facts — but only after the chain verifies, never for a month that has not finished, and never as an empty seal; and because a seal is the only thing that can catch a row *deleted* from an old month (a chain describes the rows that are there, not the ones that are gone), `--archives` re-reads every sealed segment and names the one that no longer matches. A third real defect fell out of the same test wave: six of the twelve printable types were gated behind permission keys that were never checked against the seeder — one of them, `sales.payments.print`, did not exist at all — so a census test now walks the renderer catalogue against the `permissions` table and the missing key was seeded. Everything else in §16 — search, the notification centre, BI, the API surface, backups and security alerts (16-36, whose severity list is already written) — is still to come.

**Then the warranty register, and the delivery event that feeds it (§16-16, §16-17, §16-18).** A warranty is the one promise in this application that a customer can hold the company to in court, so three rules carry the slice. **A promise exists before it can be applied.** Cover comes from the product's own policy row, and a product with no policy is delivered with no cover at all — the register says "no policy" rather than inventing twelve months, because a default invented by software is a promise the company never made and will be asked to honour. **The promise attaches to a delivery, exactly once.** Activation happens inside the delivery event's own transaction, keyed on the delivered line (`UNIQUE (company, source_line_key)`), so a re-delivered challan or a retried request writes nothing: the test proves it by walking a challan back to `dispatched` and delivering it again, and one row and one activation event is the answer. Where no challan carried the goods — a counter sale, a service invoice — the invoice is the delivery event, and it refuses to cover a line the challan already covered, because a second promise is not a second year. **The dates are fixed when cover starts.** `starts_on` is the day the goods reached the customer and `ends_on` is that day plus the policy's months, clamped: a one-month cover begun on 31 January ends on 28 February, and on 29 February in a leap year, because Carbon's plain `addMonth()` would roll into March — a longer promise than the company made, and exactly the detail a customer notices first. Editing a product's policy afterwards changes future covers and moves nothing that was already granted, which is what makes the register a record rather than a view of current settings. The clock, not the column, answers "is this covered": `status` keeps the life cycle and `stateNow()` compares `ends_on` with today, so a cover that ran out yesterday reads as expired even though the nightly job has not run — the same reason the register opens on the live lens. On top of that, **a claim never edits the cover**: it records the fault, and closing it means stating what was done (repaired, replaced, refunded — or refused), by whom and at what cost, while a cover that has run out or was withdrawn refuses a claim outright, because a goodwill repair is an expense rather than a warranty. The §07 catalogue leaves (`Warranty Returns | Processing | Completed`) and `Product Warranty Config` are pinned to the real screens with the lenses they name. One integration fell out of the work rather than being planned: the public verification page (§16-19/§16-20) had been printing "warranty periods are not registered in this system" — true when it was written, false the moment the register existed — so it now names the date the cover runs to, or ended on, and only falls back to saying no period is registered when that is what the data says. |
